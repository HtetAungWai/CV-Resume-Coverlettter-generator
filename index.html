Three documents, three jobs

People mix these up. Sending the wrong one is the first mistake a recruiter notices.

Resume

What it is
A tight summary of your best, most relevant experience and skills.
Length
One to two pages at most; one page is ideal for students.
Use it for
Most jobs and internships, especially in tech and business.
Changes
Tailor it for every role.

CV (curriculum vitae)

What it is
A complete record of your education, projects, research, awards and certifications.
Length
No fixed limit: often 1–2 pages early on, longer as your record grows.
Use it for
Academia, research, scholarships, and some countries where “CV” means resume.
Changes
Add to it; reorder to suit the reader.

Cover letter

What it is
A short letter explaining why you want this role at this company, and why you fit.
Length
Half to three quarters of a page, three or four paragraphs.
Use it for
Alongside a resume or CV, whenever an application allows it.
Changes
Write a new one every time.
Rule of thumb: the resume shows what you did, the cover letter shows why it matters to this employer, and the CV is the full archive you pull from.

Resume vs CV at a glance

Resume CV
Name origin French for “summary” Latin for “course of life”
Purpose Win a job by quickly showing you fit this role Give an objective record of your academic and professional record
Used for Job and internship applications Academic, research, scholarship and programme applications
Length 1–2 pages, no more No minimum or maximum
Tailoring Rewrite for each role Keep complete; choose what to emphasise per reader
Style Short, punchy, skimmable Plain, well organised, easy to follow

How long should it be?

Resume1–2 pages
Early CV1–2 pages
Growing CV3–6 pages
Senior CVno limit, as long as the record needs

Recruiters screen hundreds of candidates, so a six-page document sent for a job application tends to go unread. Match the length to the reader.

Anatomy of a CV

Most CVs use five headings. Open each one to see what belongs there. Inside every section, put the newest item first.

Easy-to-read font such as Arial or HelveticaSize 11–12 ptMargins 0.5–1 inch, all consistentGo easy on bold, italics and bulletsProofread for typos
Personal information

Your name, title and contact details: address, email and phone number.

Leave out: ID or social security numbers, marital status, nationality, sexual orientation and religious beliefs. Local norms differ, so check what employers in your country expect.

Education and awards

Put your latest degree first with the graduation date, or the expected date. Add your thesis if you have an advanced degree. Scholarships and grants go here too.

Trim as you grow: school-era honours start to look like filler once you’re further along. Ask who is reading and what they care about.

Experience

Group entries if it helps (work, research, volunteering). For each one say what you did, where, for how long, and who supervised you in research or shadowing. The point is to show traits, not claim them: don’t write “hard-working”, describe work that proves it.

Tech students: add a Projects group with links to GitHub or a live demo.

Never exaggerate. Everything on your document is fair game in an interview, and references can be checked.

Extracurricular activities

Clubs, leadership and events you helped organise. State your role, how long you were involved and roughly how many hours a week you gave. If you started something from nothing, verbs like “founded” or “established” fit.

Publications and presentations

Group by type: peer-reviewed papers, other publications, talks, posters. With very little to list, make it a subheading under research.

Skip padding: routine class presentations don’t count, and a project should be listed once even if it became a poster and a paper.

Optional extras

Certifications, or societies and interest groups. The same rules apply: organised, short, no repeats, no fluff.

Write it well

Strong documents come from how you describe things, often more than from what you did.

Level up one bullet

Action verbs that convey “doing”

Lead: led, founded, organised, coordinated, mentoredBuild: built, designed, developed, launched, automatedImprove: improved, reduced, streamlined, increasedInvestigate: investigated, analysed, tested, surveyedShare: presented, taught, wrote, trained

Show, don’t say

Claim Evidence
Hard-working Kept a 3.6 GPA while working 15 hours a week at a campus lab
Good leader Led 6 officers to run four workshops in one semester
Team player Co-built a Telegram bot with 3 teammates, each owning one workflow

A cover letter in four short parts

1 · Hook

Name the role and one sharp reason you want this company.

2 · Proof

One or two achievements with numbers.

3 · Fit

What you’d bring to their team and goals.

4 · Close

A clear next step, then thanks.
Truth rule: never lie or inflate. If you can’t talk about it confidently in an interview, don’t put it on the page.
